The CIA Triad: Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability

The CIA Triad is one of the most fundamental models in information security. It describes three core objectives that security controls are designed to protect:

Confidentiality — information should only be accessible to authorised people and systems.

Integrity — information and systems should remain accurate, complete and protected from unauthorised modification.

Availability — information and systems should be accessible when authorised users need them.

Almost every cybersecurity control can be linked, directly or indirectly, to one or more of these objectives.

Understanding the CIA Triad is therefore useful far beyond certification exams. It provides a simple way to analyse security incidents, assess risks, select controls and understand what an organisation is actually trying to protect.

Why the CIA Triad matters

Security is not simply about preventing hackers from accessing systems.

An organisation may suffer serious harm even when no confidential information is stolen.

Consider three different incidents:

An attacker steals a customer database.
An attacker secretly changes bank account details.
An attacker takes an online banking platform offline.

All three are cybersecurity incidents, but they affect different security objectives.

Incident Primary security objective affected
Customer data is stolen Confidentiality
Financial records are altered Integrity
Online service becomes unavailable Availability

This is why the CIA Triad is such a useful analytical model.

When considering any system, ask three questions:

Who should be able to see this information?

How do we know the information has not been changed improperly?

Will authorised users be able to access it when they need it?

Those three questions correspond directly to confidentiality, integrity and availability.

1. Confidentiality
What is confidentiality?

Confidentiality means preventing unauthorised disclosure of information.

Only authorised individuals, processes or systems should be able to access particular information.

Confidentiality applies to information:

stored on devices
stored in databases
transmitted across networks
processed by applications
printed on paper
discussed verbally
stored in backups
held by third parties

It therefore extends well beyond encryption.

Examples of confidential information

Examples may include:

passwords
authentication tokens
customer records
medical records
payment-card information
bank account details
employee records
intellectual property
trade secrets
government-classified information
security architecture documentation
cryptographic keys

The amount of protection required depends on the sensitivity and value of the information.

Threats to confidentiality

Confidentiality can be compromised in many ways.

Credential theft

An attacker obtains a user's password and uses it to access information.

Phishing

A user is manipulated into revealing credentials or sensitive information.

Excessive permissions

Employees can access information that they do not genuinely need for their role.

Network interception

Traffic passing across an insecure network is intercepted.

Misconfigured cloud storage

A storage bucket containing confidential information is accidentally made public.

Lost or stolen devices

A laptop or phone containing sensitive information is lost without appropriate encryption.

Insider threats

An authorised employee deliberately accesses or distributes information they should not use for that purpose.

Shoulder surfing

Someone observes a user's screen, keyboard or confidential documents.

Confidentiality is therefore both a technical and human problem.

Controls that protect confidentiality

Common controls include:

Encryption

Encryption transforms readable information into a form that cannot normally be understood without the appropriate cryptographic key.

Encryption may protect:

data at rest — stored information
data in transit — information travelling across a network

Examples include full-disk encryption and TLS.

Authentication

Authentication establishes the identity of a user or system.

Examples include:

passwords
smart cards
certificates
biometrics
multifactor authentication

Authentication answers the question:

Who are you?

Authorisation

After identity has been established, authorisation determines what that identity is permitted to access.

For example:

A database administrator may be authorised to manage a database, while an ordinary employee may only be permitted to view specific records.

Least privilege

Users and systems should receive only the permissions necessary to perform their function.

Reducing unnecessary access reduces the potential impact of compromised accounts and insider threats.

Need-to-know

Even if someone has appropriate security clearance or general authorisation, they should only receive access to information required for their role.

Data classification

Organisations classify information according to sensitivity.

For example:

Public
Internal
Confidential
Restricted

The classification then helps determine which security controls should be applied.

Data Loss Prevention

Data Loss Prevention (DLP) technologies can detect and prevent sensitive information from leaving authorised environments.

For example, a DLP system might block an employee from emailing a spreadsheet containing thousands of credit-card numbers.

Confidentiality example

Imagine an employee database containing:

names
addresses
salaries
bank details
National Insurance numbers

An attacker gains access and downloads the database.

The database remains available.

The records have not been modified.

However, unauthorised people now possess the information.

The primary impact is therefore:

Loss of confidentiality.

2. Integrity
What is integrity?

Integrity means protecting information and systems from unauthorised or improper modification or destruction.

Information should remain:

accurate
complete
consistent
trustworthy

Integrity is essential because information can become dangerous when it cannot be trusted.

Consider a bank balance.

Protecting the confidentiality of the balance is important.

But ensuring that nobody can secretly change:

£1,000

to

£100,000

may be even more important.

Integrity includes accidental changes

Integrity violations do not always involve attackers.

Integrity can also be damaged by:

software bugs
database corruption
hardware failure
human error
incorrect configuration
failed updates
transmission errors

Security controls must therefore protect against both malicious and accidental modification.

Threats to integrity

Examples include:

Malware

Malware alters files or system configurations.

SQL injection

An attacker manipulates a vulnerable application to change database information.

Man-in-the-middle attacks

An attacker intercepts and alters communication between two parties.

Privilege abuse

An employee changes records without authorisation.

Configuration errors

An administrator accidentally alters a critical system setting.

Supply-chain compromise

Malicious code is introduced into legitimate software before customers install it.

Controls that protect integrity
Hashing

A cryptographic hash function produces a fixed-length value derived from data.

If the underlying data changes, the resulting hash should also change.

Hashes can therefore help determine whether information has been modified.

For example:

Original file
↓
Hash function
↓
Hash value

Later:

Downloaded file
↓
Hash function
↓
Compare hash

If the values differ, the file has changed.

Digital signatures

Digital signatures can provide assurance that:

information has not been modified
the message originated from the expected signer

They therefore support both integrity and authentication.

File Integrity Monitoring

File Integrity Monitoring systems detect changes to important files and configurations.

They may alert security teams if critical operating-system or application files are modified unexpectedly.

Access control

Preventing unauthorised users from modifying information is a fundamental integrity control.

For example, many users might have permission to read financial records while only a small number can modify them.

Change management

Formal change-management processes help ensure system modifications are:

authorised
tested
documented
reviewed
reversible where necessary

This protects integrity from accidental as well as malicious changes.

Version control

Version control systems provide a history of changes to source code and other information.

They make it easier to identify:

who made a change
when it occurred
exactly what changed
how to restore an earlier version
Backups

Backups are usually associated with availability, but they can also help restore information whose integrity has been compromised.

For example, if ransomware modifies thousands of files, a known-good backup can restore accurate versions.

This illustrates an important principle:

Security controls often support more than one element of the CIA Triad.

Integrity example

Imagine an attacker compromises an online retailer.

Instead of stealing customer records, the attacker changes the company's bank account number on invoices so that customers send payments to the attacker.

The information is still accessible.

Nobody necessarily sees information they were not authorised to see.

But the information has been maliciously altered.

The primary impact is:

Loss of integrity.

3. Availability
What is availability?

Availability means ensuring that authorised users can access systems, services and information when required.

A perfectly confidential and accurate system is of little value if nobody can use it.

Availability is particularly critical for systems such as:

hospitals
emergency services
financial systems
payment platforms
telecommunications
transport systems
industrial control systems
cloud services

In some environments, availability can directly affect human safety.

Threats to availability
Denial-of-Service attacks

Attackers deliberately overwhelm systems or network connections.

A Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) attack uses many systems simultaneously to generate traffic.

Ransomware

Ransomware may encrypt or disable information and systems, preventing legitimate users from accessing them.

Ransomware can therefore affect both:

Availability and integrity.

If information is also stolen before encryption, confidentiality is affected as well.

Hardware failure

Hard drives, power supplies, network equipment and other components can fail.

Power outage

Systems may become unavailable if electrical power is interrupted.

Natural disasters

Floods, fires, earthquakes and severe weather can damage infrastructure.

Human error

An administrator might accidentally delete data or misconfigure a production service.

Capacity problems

Even legitimate demand can overwhelm systems that were not designed with sufficient capacity.

Controls that protect availability
Redundancy

Critical components can be duplicated so that failure of one component does not cause total service failure.

Examples include:

redundant network links
multiple servers
redundant power supplies
multiple data centres
High availability

High-availability architectures aim to minimise service interruption when individual components fail.

Load balancing

Traffic can be distributed across multiple systems rather than relying on a single server.

Backups

Backups allow information to be restored following:

deletion
corruption
ransomware
hardware failure
disaster

However, simply having a backup is not enough.

Backups should also be tested to ensure that restoration actually works.

Disaster recovery

Disaster recovery planning provides procedures and technology for restoring systems after major disruption.

Business continuity

Business continuity considers how the organisation continues delivering critical business functions during disruption.

Disaster recovery is often focused more specifically on recovering technology.

DDoS protection

Traffic filtering, content delivery networks and specialised anti-DDoS services can help absorb or block malicious traffic.

Monitoring

Monitoring systems can identify failures or degradation before they become major outages.

Patch and maintenance programmes

Although patching is often associated with preventing compromise, good maintenance also improves reliability and availability.

Availability example

A company experiences a DDoS attack that prevents customers from accessing its website for six hours.

No customer information is stolen.

No records are modified.

The primary problem is that legitimate users cannot access the service.

The primary impact is:

Loss of availability.

Security incidents can affect more than one element

Real-world incidents rarely fit perfectly into a single category.

Consider ransomware.

An attacker might:

steal confidential information,
modify or encrypt files,
make systems unavailable.

That means the attack could affect:

Confidentiality — data was stolen
Integrity — data was altered
Availability — systems cannot be used

Understanding the CIA Triad is therefore not about forcing every incident into one box.

It is about identifying which security properties have been affected.

Balancing confidentiality, integrity and availability

Security decisions frequently involve trade-offs.

For example, imagine a hospital emergency system.

Extremely restrictive authentication procedures might increase confidentiality, but requiring doctors to complete five separate authentication steps during an emergency could reduce availability and negatively affect patient care.

Similarly, making information extremely easy to access might improve availability while weakening confidentiality.

Good security architecture therefore tries to achieve an appropriate balance based on:

business requirements
risk
information sensitivity
regulatory obligations
operational needs
safety requirements

There is no universal rule saying that one element of the CIA Triad is always more important than the others.

The appropriate priority depends on the system.

Different systems have different priorities
Online banking

All three elements are extremely important.

Confidentiality

Customers should not see each other's financial information.

Integrity

Account balances and transactions must be accurate.

Availability

Customers need reliable access to banking services.

Public news website

Confidentiality of published articles may not be particularly important because the content is deliberately public.

However:

Integrity is important because attackers should not be able to alter published stories.

Availability is important because readers need to reach the site.

Military intelligence database

Confidentiality may be exceptionally important because disclosure could threaten national security.

Integrity is also critical because commanders need accurate information.

Emergency medical system

Availability may receive particularly high priority because delays could endanger patients.

This demonstrates why security decisions must consider the business and operational context.

CIA Triad and risk assessment

The CIA Triad can also help when assessing risk.

For each asset, ask:

Confidentiality

What would happen if unauthorised people obtained this information?

Integrity

What would happen if this information became inaccurate or maliciously altered?

Availability

What would happen if this system or information became unavailable?

The answers help determine the potential impact of a security event.

For example:

Asset Confidentiality Integrity Availability
Public marketing website Low High Medium
Payroll database High High Medium
Emergency medical system High High Very High
Public press release Low High Low/Medium

The exact classifications will depend on the organisation and context.

CIA Triad and security controls

It is useful to practise identifying which objectives different controls support.

Security control C I A
Encryption ✓
Access control ✓ ✓
Hashing ✓
Digital signatures ✓
Backups ✓ ✓
Redundant servers ✓
DDoS protection ✓
Multifactor authentication ✓ ✓
File integrity monitoring ✓
Data Loss Prevention ✓

This table is deliberately simplified.

Many controls affect multiple security objectives depending on how they are implemented.

Related security concepts

The CIA Triad is foundational, but it does not describe every desirable security property.

You will also encounter concepts such as:

Authenticity

Confidence that a user, system, message or piece of information is genuine.

Accountability

The ability to associate actions with a particular user or entity.

Logging and auditing are important accountability controls.

Non-repudiation

Providing evidence so that an individual cannot credibly deny performing a particular action.

Digital signatures can contribute to non-repudiation.

Privacy

Privacy concerns the appropriate collection, processing, storage and use of personal information.

Privacy and confidentiality overlap, but they are not exactly the same concept.

A useful opposite: the DAD Triad

A helpful way to remember the CIA Triad is to consider three things an attacker might try to achieve.

This is sometimes described as the DAD Triad:

Disclosure attacks confidentiality.

Alteration attacks integrity.

Destruction or Denial attacks availability.

So:

Confidentiality ↔ Disclosure


Integrity ↔ Alteration


Availability ↔ Destruction/Denial

This can be particularly useful when analysing exam questions or security scenarios.

Common mistakes
Mistake 1: Assuming encryption provides integrity

Encryption primarily protects confidentiality.

Some cryptographic mechanisms can provide integrity as well, but encryption alone should not automatically be treated as an integrity control.

Mistake 2: Thinking integrity means secrecy

Integrity concerns accuracy and protection from unauthorised modification.

Confidentiality concerns secrecy and unauthorised disclosure.

Mistake 3: Thinking availability means simply being online

Availability also means that authorised users can reliably access required information and services within acceptable timescales.

A service that technically responds but is unusably slow may still have an availability problem.

Mistake 4: Assuming every control maps to one objective

Controls often support multiple objectives.

Backups, for example, support availability but can also help restore the integrity of damaged information.

CISSP exam perspective

When a CISSP-style question describes an incident, identify what actually happened to the asset.

Look for clues such as:

Confidentiality
exposed
disclosed
leaked
intercepted
stolen
viewed by unauthorised people
Integrity
modified
manipulated
corrupted
altered
falsified
tampered with
Availability
inaccessible
offline
unavailable
disrupted
destroyed
overwhelmed

For example:

An attacker intercepts a database backup containing customer information but does not modify it.

Primary concern:

Confidentiality

An employee changes financial transactions without authorisation.

Primary concern:

Integrity

A DDoS attack prevents customers from accessing an application.

Primary concern:

Availability

Quick memory aid

Think:

Confidentiality = Who can SEE it?
Integrity = Can I TRUST it?
Availability = Can I REACH it?

Or simply:

Secret. Correct. Available.

Key takeaways

The CIA Triad consists of three fundamental security objectives:

Confidentiality
Protect information from unauthorised disclosure.

Integrity
Protect information and systems from unauthorised or improper modification.

Availability
Ensure authorised users can access information and services when required.

The model is simple, but extremely powerful.

It can be used to:

understand security incidents
assess business impact
identify appropriate controls
analyse threats
classify information
design systems
reason through certification exam questions

Most importantly, security is rarely about maximising one objective at all costs.

Effective security requires an appropriate balance between confidentiality, integrity and availability based on the needs and risks of the organisation.
